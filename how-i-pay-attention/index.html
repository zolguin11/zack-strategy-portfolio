How I Work

One of the main things I’m trying to figure out when in a new environment is the answer to the question, “what are these people like to work with?” I assume you’re here trying to figure that out too. So, I’d like to use this page to give a little insight into what it’s like to work with me.

What I Listen For

When I am learning a new workplace, client, audience, or problem, my process typically begins with asking a lot of questions. I try to understand the situation from several directions and pay close attention to the answers I hear.

I usually try to keep this just shy of a really annoying amount (at least the folks I’ve worked with haven’t complained yet). My goal is to understand the environment, understand how information moves, look for patterns and possible explanations, turn that finding into something other people can use.

Here are some of the questions I ask:

First, understand the environment

  • What is the team responsible for?
  • What decisions are clients trying to make?
  • How do analysts, strategists, digital teams, and account teams work together?
  • What does each group need from research?
  • What makes a report useful to them?
  • What kinds of findings get acted on?
  • What kinds of findings get ignored?

Then, understand how information moves

  • Where does the information come from?
  • How is it collected?
  • How is it organized?
  • Where does human judgment enter the process?
  • What gets removed or simplified before it reaches the final report?
  • Are there patterns that everyone sees but nobody has had time to explore?
  • Are teams tracking something because it matters, or because it is available and easy to measure?

Then, look for patterns and possible explanations

  • What keeps repeating?
  • What changed?
  • What seems unusually high or low?
  • What groups are behaving differently?
  • What language is appearing more often?
  • What happened around the same time?
  • What are the obvious explanations?
  • What are the less obvious ones?
  • What information would help distinguish between them?

Finally, turn the finding into something other people can use

  • What is actually happening?
  • How certain are we?
  • Why might it be happening?
  • Who does it matter to?
  • What decision could it affect?
  • What should someone do next?
  • What should we continue watching?

What I Believe About Insight

01

Data does not interpret itself

Data can show that something is happening, but it rarely explains the full reason why.

That takes interpretation, context, and some understanding of the people represented in the data. It also means checking whether an explanation actually holds up, or whether it is simply plausible enough to sound convincing.

02

People are more complicated than their category

Demographics and audience segments can be useful, but they are starting points. I look at both numbers and the person behind the number.

Two people with the same age, income, location, or job title may value completely different things and may arrive at the same choice for different reasons. I want to understand the person behind the category rather than letting the category become the explanation.

03

Clear information gives people more control over their choices

Unclear or misleading information bothers me because people make real decisions from it.

The less honest and specific the information is, the harder it becomes for someone to understand their options and make a choice they actually feel confident in.

04

A useful finding should lead somewhere

Sometimes a finding changes a decision. Sometimes it challenges an assumption, explains a behavior more clearly, or shows that the team needs to ask a better question before acting.

I don't think every observation needs to end with a dramatic recommendation, but it should help someone understand the situation more accurately than they did before.

What It Is Like to Work With Me

I can't fully describe what it is like to work with me from my own perspective, but these are some patterns I recognize in how I show up.

I ask a lot of questions. Usually I am trying to understand why something exists, whether it is actually working, who it is serving, and what information might change the answer.

I may be quieter at first. I am usually observing how people communicate, what the group values, and where I can be useful. Once I understand the environment, I am much more likely to contribute ideas, share observations, or suggest a different way forward.

I connect things quickly. I often notice overlap between ideas, behaviors, systems, and information that arrived from different places.

I care about making work easier. I naturally look for repeated friction, unnecessary steps, and information that could be made clearer or more useful.

I don't believe people are interchangeable. One person may create clear plans. Another may adapt easily when things change. Another may make sure everyone feels included. I think teams are stronger when people are given room to contribute through their strengths rather than being treated like copies of the same ideal employee.

I take work seriously without needing everything to feel serious. I have generally done my best work in environments where people can be thoughtful and rigorous while still having feeling socially safe enough to be silly, curious, joyful, and earnest.

What This Looks Like in Practice

At High Point University, I managed recruitment strategy for Virginia. Enrollment data showed that students from private schools were 50% more likely to enroll than students from public schools.

That pattern was useful, but it did not explain itself.

  1. What I noticed:

    Private-school students were enrolling at a higher rate.

  2. The easy conclusion:

    Target more private schools.

    That was directionally useful, but it was not enough to explain why the pattern existed or how we should communicate with those families.

  3. The questions it raised:

    Were those families more familiar with private education?
    Was income contributing to the result?

    Did they understand the university's value more quickly?

    Were parents more involved in the choice?

    Were they comparing High Point against a different set of schools?
    What do those students and parents value?

  4. What I considered together:

    I looked at enrollment data alongside family conversations, school context, common objections, parent involvement, SES, and the differences I was hearing across the territory. No single source contained the full answer.

  5. How it changed the work:

    The pattern helped me prioritize where I spent time, while the context shaped how I communicated.

    It led to more focused school outreach and more specific conversations about value, trust, fit, prestige, and what different families needed in order to feel confident moving forward. Virginia applications rose by 27%.

What I Would Bring

I would not arrive already knowing every platform, client, methodology, or internal way of working, but I would arrive ready to learn them carefully, ask useful questions, notice what deserves a closer look, and help turn a large amount of information into a clearer understanding of what may be happening and why.
